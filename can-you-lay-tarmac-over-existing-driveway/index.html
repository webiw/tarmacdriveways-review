Can You Get Away With Just Laying New Tarmac Over an Existing Driveway?

If your driveway is looking a bit worse for wear, faded, cracked or what have you, it’s probably occurred to you that just laying new tarmac on top would be a great way to sort it out. It’s cheaper, faster, and less hassle than ripping it all out from scratch. And in a lot of cases it actually works just fine. But, there are plenty of times when it just hides a problem that’s waiting to come back.

The short answer is yes, you can lay new tarmac over an existing driveway – but only if the surface underneath is solid, level enough and draining properly. And it’s that last bit that usually decides whether a job is going to be a success.

And that’s where a lot of homeowners end up getting caught out – they put down a new surface over a weak base and it all looks fine for a season or so – but then the trouble comes back. Below is how to work out which camp your driveway falls into, and when tarmac resurfacing in Dublin is the right call rather than a full rebuild.

Yes, But It Has to Be the Right Kind of Driveway

An overlay is just a new layer of tarmac laid on top of the old surface. The thing is, the finished driveway is only ever as good as what’s sitting underneath it. So the existing driveway has to be up to scratch.

For an overlay to work, the old surface should:

  • Be structurally sound – no soft or sinking bits
  • Be reasonably level, or level enough to fix with the new layer
  • Be draining properly, so water runs off rather than pooling
  • Be free of deep cracks, potholes and crumbling edges

If your driveway ticks all those boxes, an overlay will probably give you another few years out of it – for a fraction of the cost of replacing it. If it doesn’t, laying tarmac on top is just delaying the inevitable – same problem, new colour.

When a New Tarmac Overlay is a Good Option

Plenty of driveways are perfectly fine underneath but just look a bit worn out. That’s exactly what an overlay is for.

It tends to work best when the surface is:

  • Faded or grey but still solid
  • Lightly cracked with just surface-level cracks
  • Looking a bit rough or patchy but still stable
  • Structurally sound, with no dips or movement underfoot

A driveway like that usually has a base that’s still doing its job. The surface has just had a bit of a bad time. In that situation a new tarmac overlay brings the whole entrance back up to scratch, smooths out minor wear and gives you a clean finish without all the hassle of digging everything up. For most tidy suburban driveways that have just lost their looks, this is the sensible thing to do.

When Overlay is Not Recommended

The honest truth is that covering up a failing driveway with new tarmac is usually just a waste of money. If the base has gone, no surface layer is going to make much difference.

Avoid an overlay if your driveway has:

  • Sinking or dipping bits, which means the sub-base is failing
  • Potholes, which usually means the base has broken up
  • Deep or wide cracks – the sort that reopen after you’ve tried to fill them
  • Water pooling after rain – a sign that the drainage isn’t working
  • Loose, crumbling edges with no solid support
  • A soft or spongy feel underfoot – that’s a sure sign the problem is under the surface

Any of those tell you the trouble is underneath, not on top. Lay new tarmac over a base that’s moving around and the movement will just carry right on up into your new surface. Within a couple of winters you’ll be right back where you started – only this time you’ll have paid twice. Why the Base Matters More Than the Surface

It’s easy to get caught up in focusing on the black surface you can see. But it’s the sub-base underneath – the compacted stone layer that spreads the weight of every car that goes over it – that actually keeps the driveway alive.

A good sub-base does all the hard work – it carries the load, resists movement, and stops the surface from flexing and cracking under the weight of the traffic. When a driveway fails early on, it’s nearly always the base that’s the problem – not the tarmac on top.

That’s the difference between a good contractor and a bloke with a roller and a lot of enthusiasm. Anyone can lay a smooth black surface on a dry day. But knowing whether the base can take it – and being honest about it – is where the skill lies. If the base is solid, an overlay is probably a good option. If it’s not, the honest answer is that you need more than just a new surface.

Making Sure the Drainage is Right Before Laying New Tarmac

Water is the thing that quietly sets about destroying driveways, and in Dublin, where the rain comes down in sheets, there’s no shortage of it. Before you even think about laying a new layer of tarmac, the drainage needs a proper check.

The key thing is the drainage fall – the slight slope that sends water away from the house, garage and entrance. And you change the levels when you put on a new layer of tarmac. So get the fall wrong and you could end up sending water the opposite way to what you want – straight towards the house, in fact. Not a problem you want to have.

A good contractor should check:

  • The existing falls and how the new surface changes them
  • Any drainage channels or linear drains running across the entrance
  • Gullies and where surface water tends to pool
  • Manhole and inspection covers, which need to sit flush with the new finished level
  • Whether water is running directly toward the house, or your garage, or your neighbours

If your driveway is already prone to puddling, slapping a layer of tarmac on top is NOT the answer. You’ve got to sort the falls and the drainage first. Our handy guide to driveway drainage goes into more detail on this – and trust us, it’s a step you shouldn’t skip.

Tarmac Overlay vs Full Resurfacing vs Replacing the Whole Driveway

We’ve all seen people bandy about these terms, but what do they really mean?

You’d think the cheapest option would be the way to go, but it’s not always that simple. The thing is, it’s not just about the cost – it’s about what’s going on underneath. Paying for an overlay when the base is shot is not going to save you money in the long run – it’ll just mean you have to do it all again.

What a Contractor Does to an Old Driveway Before Laying New Tarmac

Now, what makes an overlay last? Well, preparation is key. Laying tarmac over a dusty, cracked, un-swept surface is just asking for trouble down the line.

A typical, proper prep job for an overlay is:

  1. Cleaning the existing surface from top to bottom, getting rid of moss, dirt and all that loose stuff
  2. Cutting back and tidying up any weak or crumbling edges
  3. Fixing any potholes, dips or soft bits so the layer underneath is sound
  4. Levelling out any low spots and sorting out those minor falls
  5. Slapping on a tack coat (and don’t even get us started on how important that is) – that’s the bonding layer that glues the new tarmac to the old
  6. Laying the new surface layer down at the right depth
  7. Rolling it out and compacting it so it’s good to go for cars

The tack coat is the bit people often overlook, but trust us, it makes all the difference. And don’t even get us started on compaction – that’s the rolling that firms everything up and turns fresh tarmac into a surface that can take a beating, not just a scuff and shove.

Should You Get Your Driveway Inspected Before You Decide?

If you’re not sure which camp your driveway falls into, that’s exactly when you need to get it looked at. It’s not always obvious just from looking, or even from standing on it.

A quick site check will tell you the important questions: is the base still sound, is the drainage working, and will an overlay actually fix the problem or just postpone it? Trust us, it’s way cheaper to do it this way than to pay for a surface that’s only going to fail within a year. Frequently Asked Questions

Can You Put New Tarmac Over Old Tarmac?
Yeah, but only if the old driveway is still structurally sound and has decent drainage. If the old tarmac is stable and just looking a bit worn, an overlay is fine. But if it’s sinking, got potholes or collecting water, new tarmac on top will not fix the underlying problem.
Can Tarmac Be Laid Over Concrete?
Sometimes, but it depends on the state of the concrete base – levels, drainage, movement and all that. Cracked or shifting concrete can show through in the new tarmac, so the base needs a good check first. A tack coat and proper prep are essential if it’s suitable.
Is an Overlay Always Cheaper Than Replacing a Driveway?

Usually, because it avoids all the excavation and getting rid of the old base. But it’s only a genuine saving when the existing base is still good. Overlaying a failing driveway tends to cost more in the long run, as the problem just comes back and you’ve got to do it all again.

How Thick Should a Tarmac Overlay Be?

Thickness depends on the condition of the existing surface, and how it’s used – domestic or commercial use, that kind of thing. A contractor will work that out when they inspect the surface – if it’s too thin, it won’t last.
